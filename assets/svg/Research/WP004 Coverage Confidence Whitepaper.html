ABSTRACT

This paper documents pre-build validation research for a Coverage vs Confidence module planned as a future feature addition to AI Spider, a proprietary locally-hosted website crawler and AI retrievability audit platform. It is the fourth paper in a series validating the planned content intelligence layer of AI Spider alongside WP001 (Competitive Content Intelligence), WP002 (Coverage Score and Entity Expansion), and WP003 (Retrieval Drift Analysis).

Each previous module measured a single dimension: what is missing (WP001), how complete (WP002), how focused (WP003). This module introduces a fundamentally different structure - it evaluates two independent dimensions simultaneously. Coverage measures how completely a page addresses the expected topic. Confidence measures how well its claims are supported by evidence. A page can score high on one and low on the other, and the gap between them carries its own actionable signal.

Research involved designing a five-stage prompt with a deterministic signal audit embedded in Stage 3, building a two-vertical test set (SaaS pricing pages and AI/GEO tools), establishing manual ground truth for both dimensions and both verticals, and running the identical prompt across Claude Sonnet 4.6, ChatGPT GPT-5.5, and Gemini 3.5 Flash.

Research Conclusion

Coverage vs Confidence analysis is a valid and discriminating signal for AI retrievability. All three frontier models correctly maintained the two dimensions as independent measurements and correctly classified both target pages into their expected matrix quadrants. The most important finding is that Claude and ChatGPT produced materially different Confidence scores for the same page due to different signal counting methodologies - Claude counted 20 signals for monday.com (treating plan limits as evidence of specificity) while ChatGPT counted 26. Both are defensible. This counting variability has direct implications for how the module should present Confidence scores. Gemini under-generated expected topics in Stage 1 (consistent with WP002) but correctly maintained dimension independence throughout.

SECTION 1
Background and Research Motivation

Working Papers 001 through 003 established that content gap analysis, coverage scoring, and retrieval drift measurement are each valid signals for AI retrievability improvement. Each module answers a different question about a page's relationship to its query. But all three share a common limitation: they evaluate what is present or absent on a page, not whether what is present is actually believable.

"A page can cover every topic a user might ask about and still fail to be cited by an AI model - not because it is missing information, but because none of its claims are backed by numbers, named sources, testimonials, or evidence that an AI model can treat as reliable enough to surface."

This is the problem the Coverage vs Confidence module addresses. It separates two independent measurements that existing tools - including the modules validated in WP001-003 - conflate. Coverage is a completeness question: does the page contain what a full answer requires? Confidence is a credibility question: are the claims on the page supported by verifiable evidence?

The distinction matters because AI models do not just retrieve pages that are topically complete. They weight retrieval toward pages that appear trustworthy - pages that contain specific numbers, named sources, dated research, customer testimonials with quantified outcomes, and third-party validation. A page that covers every topic but cites nothing scores high on Coverage and low on Confidence. A page that covers only one topic but provides a Gartner citation, G2 rating, and three named case studies scores low on Coverage and high on Confidence.

Both patterns are actionable in different ways. High Coverage / Low Confidence pages need evidence injection - testimonials, statistics, source citations. Low Coverage / High Confidence pages need topic expansion without diluting the evidence density they already have. The 2x2 matrix is the core output of this module, and each quadrant maps to a different remediation strategy.

Before building the module, three research questions needed validation:

  • Q1Can LLMs maintain Coverage and Confidence as genuinely independent dimensions? Or do models assume that low coverage implies low confidence, collapsing the two into a single quality judgment?
  • Q2Is the deterministic signal audit reliable? Stage 3 asks models to count specific evidence signals - numbers, testimonials, source references, tables. Do models agree on what counts and how many exist?
  • Q3Does the matrix quadrant classification match ground truth? The module's primary output is quadrant placement. Do all three models agree on which quadrant each target page belongs in?

Research Methodology

2.2
The Five-Stage Prompt

The module uses a five-stage prompt - one more than WP003 and two more than WP002. Stage 3 introduces the deterministic signal audit: a structured enumeration of objective evidence signals that can be counted and verified independently of model judgment. This is the most significant methodological addition in the series.

STAGE 01
Expected topic set
Generate what a complete answer requires. Coverage ceiling.
STAGE 02
Coverage analysis
Evaluate target page against expected topics. Calculate Coverage Score.
STAGE 03
Signal audit
Count deterministic evidence signals: numbers, sources, testimonials, tables, dates.
STAGE 04
Confidence evaluation
Score specificity, evidence support, and credibility. Calculate Confidence Score.
STAGE 05
Matrix + recs
Classify quadrant. Compare competitors. Generate separate Coverage and Confidence recommendations.
Figure 2. The five-stage reasoning framework. Teal stages belong to the Coverage dimension; amber to Confidence; blue to synthesis. Stage 3 (deterministic signal audit) is the methodological signature of WP004 - it introduces an objectively verifiable layer that enables cross-model consistency checking not possible in WP001-003.

The full prompt template used across all three models is reproduced below with page content omitted for brevity.

Prompt Template - v1.0 All 3 models identical - Both verticals
Page content omitted

// SYSTEM ROLE

You are a Coverage vs Confidence Analysis engine. Evaluate the TARGET page across TWO INDEPENDENT dimensions.

COVERAGE: How completely the page addresses the expected topic set.
CONFIDENCE: How well the page's claims are supported by evidence.

These are INDEPENDENT. High coverage does not imply high confidence. Do not conflate them.

// STAGE 3 - DETERMINISTIC SIGNAL AUDIT (key methodological addition)

Count and list every deterministic evidence signal:
- Numbers and statistics (specific figures, percentages, user counts)
- Named source references (According to X, cited research)
- Comparison tables (yes/no, describe)
- Customer testimonials or named quotes
- Named case studies or real-world examples
- Dates and recency signals
- Total confidence signals counted: [number]

// OUTPUT FORMAT - strictly enforced

STAGE 1 - EXPECTED TOPIC SET + STAGE 2 - COVERAGE ANALYSIS
- Expected topics [numbered] / Covered [checkmarks] / Missing [crosses] / Coverage Score %

STAGE 3 - SIGNAL AUDIT + STAGE 4 - CONFIDENCE EVALUATION
- Signal inventory per category / Total count / Confidence Score 0-100 / Rationale

STAGE 5 - MATRIX CLASSIFICATION AND RECOMMENDATIONS
- Quadrant [HIGH/LOW COVERAGE - HIGH/LOW CONFIDENCE]
- Competitor comparison (Coverage % / Confidence % for each)
- Coverage recommendations: Priority 1/2/3
- Confidence recommendations: Priority 1/2/3
Respond only with the structured report. No commentary outside the format.
Figure 3. Full prompt template. Teal = Coverage dimension. Amber = Confidence dimension. Purple = synthesis stage. The independence instruction ("These are INDEPENDENT. Do not conflate them.") was the most consequential design decision - without it, models default to assuming that low coverage implies low confidence.
Key Prompt Design Decision: The Independence Instruction

Early prototype testing revealed that without an explicit instruction, all three models conflated the two dimensions - assuming that a page with low coverage must also have low confidence. The explicit statement "High coverage does not imply high confidence. Do not conflate them" was required to force genuinely independent scoring. This instruction is the single most important structural feature of the WP004 prompt and has no equivalent in WP001-003.

SECTION 4
Key Findings

This benchmark produced six findings. Four continue the series-level patterns established in WP001-003; two are unique to the Coverage vs Confidence module.

F1
Both dimensions validated as independent signals
All three models maintained Coverage and Confidence as genuinely independent measurements across both verticals. Neither model assumed that low coverage implied low confidence. The independence instruction in the prompt was necessary to achieve this - without it, models conflated the dimensions in prototype testing.
F2
Matrix quadrant: unanimous agreement both verticals
All three models placed monday.com in HIGH/HIGH and Semrush in HIGH/LOW - matching ground truth exactly. This is the strongest validation result in the WP004 series. Despite meaningful score divergence, the classification output - which is what users act on - was identical across all models.
F3
Confidence score divergence is driven by signal counting definitions
Claude scored monday.com at 72% Confidence; ChatGPT at 83-87%; Gemini at 87%. The gap traces entirely to Stage 3: ChatGPT counted plan-tier comparisons as a "comparison table" and testimonials as "case studies," adding 6 extra signals. Both interpretations are defensible. The module must define signal categories precisely to reduce this variance.
F4
Semrush specificity vs credibility distinction correctly identified
All three models correctly distinguished between the Semrush page's specificity (granular usage limits, precise pricing) and its lack of credibility signals (zero testimonials, zero G2/Gartner, zero case studies). Claude articulated this most precisely: "technically credible but commercially unvalidated." This is exactly the insight the Confidence dimension is designed to surface.
F5
Recommendations correctly split by dimension - Coverage and Confidence produce different actions
For Semrush (HIGH Coverage / LOW Confidence), all three models produced separate Coverage recommendations (add integrations, expand platform coverage) and Confidence recommendations (add G2 rating, add testimonial with quantified outcome, add Gartner badge). These are genuinely different actions targeting different editorial problems. If the module had collapsed Coverage and Confidence into a single quality score, the recommendations would have been a mixed, unordered list. The separation produces a more actionable, structured output - and this finding validates the core premise of the module.
F6
Coverage scores systematically overstated - consistent with WP002 series finding
The Semrush KB page scored 67% (Claude), 78% (ChatGPT), and 80% (Gemini) Coverage against a ground truth of ~35-40%. This is a second instance of the WP002 finding - models interpret partial coverage of a topic as full coverage. This is not a new finding but its recurrence across both WP002 and WP004 confirms it is a systematic model behaviour requiring a post-processing calibration step, not a prompt-fixable issue.
Series finding - consistent across WP001, WP002, WP003, WP004

Across four consecutive benchmarks: Claude Sonnet 4.6 has been the most calibrated model in every test. It produced the most accurate signal counts in WP004 Stage 3, the most precise expected topic set in WP002, the best entity classification in WP003, and the sharpest recommendations in WP001. ChatGPT GPT-5.5 has been consistently production-ready with a slight tendency toward over-counting and over-inclusion. Gemini 3.5 Flash has maintained the same pattern in every paper: correct high-level output (quadrant agreement, correct drift direction, correct coverage direction) with systematic quantitative inflation (under-generated topics WP002, over-classified drift WP003, over-estimated confidence WP004). The AI Spider content intelligence suite will use Claude as the recommended default across all four modules, with documented calibration guidance for other models.

SECTION 5
Implications for AI Spider
  • 01
    Signal category definitions will be standardised before module launch
    Finding F3 established that signal counting variance is driven by definition ambiguity - specifically whether plan-tier feature comparisons count as "comparison tables" and whether testimonials count as "case studies." The module will ship with a strict signal taxonomy: six named categories with explicit inclusion and exclusion criteria. This will reduce model-to-model Confidence score variance and make scores comparable across audits.
  • 02
    Matrix quadrant displayed before scores
    Finding F2 confirms that quadrant classification is unanimous across models despite score divergence. The module UI will lead with the matrix quadrant - "Your page is HIGH Coverage / LOW Confidence" - before displaying any numeric scores. The quadrant is the actionable output; the scores are supporting detail. This is the inverse of how coverage tools typically present data.
  • 03
    Recommendations split into two separate panels by dimension
    Finding F5 confirms that Coverage and Confidence recommendations are genuinely different editorial actions. The module UI will present two separate recommendation panels: a Coverage panel (topics to add) and a Confidence panel (evidence signals to add). Mixing them into a single prioritised list would lose the analytical value of having measured the two dimensions independently.
  • 04
    Coverage score calibration layer applied consistently with WP002
    Finding F6 confirms that all three models systematically overstate coverage for this module as they did in WP002. The same post-processing calibration step planned for WP002 will apply here: a minimum topic count guard and a systematic downward adjustment to coverage scores above a threshold, with a transparency note shown to users. This is not model-specific - it applies to all three.
  • 05
    Coverage vs Confidence integrates with the full content intelligence stack
    A page identified as HIGH Coverage / LOW Confidence by this module that also shows Drift entities (WP003) and entity gaps (WP001) has a complete content intelligence profile: what it covers, what it is missing, where it drifts, and where its evidence is weak. The highest-priority recommendation across all four modules is: a missing entity (WP001) that is also a Drift contributor (WP003) and a missing confidence signal (WP004) simultaneously. AI Spider will surface this cross-module signal as the first action item in the unified recommendations panel.
